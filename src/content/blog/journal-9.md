&emsp;As the first entry of this series, I will detail the macro-scale of what I’m trying to do, what worked, and what didn’t. This series will be different from the other entries because it will contain a lot more opinions and thoughts than the other writings where I’ve used more logic to show my points. Anyways, if you are reading this, enjoy the ride of getting a view into my world and what I’ve been up to! :)

&emsp;As I am writing this entry, it is currently 2:28 a.m.(which admittedly is a bad time to be awake considering that I have a 9 a.m. class). I am still in the works of figuring out my sleep schedule as I am tacking heavy classes this semester, but I know that the work I put in to make it better will be worth it in the long run especially towards my next midterms and future finals(and decal project which I will explain in a future entry). Alongside studying for my classes (60% of the time being on math 104, which is introduction to "real" analysis), I have been reading along ML textbooks to prep for future “foreseeable” personal projects when I may be using AI models do predict physical phenomena or optimizing certain parameters of a physical system.

&emsp;Looking at the things that I have been doing currently and in the past, I have been asked these particular question a lot: “Why computer science?”, “Why physics?”, “Why pursue a degree in both?”. Firstly, I was at a decent level of coding, so I thought I might as well continue to build that skill throughout college. I have been coding on/off since 4th grade, but I was not learning at a pace where I could just do USACO(national CS completition) and place very well. A lot of that time was spent elsewhere in the development of the skills I needed to do well in the physical sciences, which includes but not limited to doing math and physics problems (not at the competition level for the former). The interesting part of my journey in pursuing physics was that I initially didn’t know that I wanted to pursue physics until sophomore year of high school when I decided to apply my physics knowledge into my computer science projects, such as using kinematics to help approximate the exact parameters to allow a robot to shoot a gamepiece into the goal. To be clear, my passion in physics didn’t only come from a single inspiration. It all started with Bill Nye the Science Guy and later Star Talk when I binge-watched a lot of astrophysics videos from Niel Degrasse Tyson and later Chuck. Through exposure to these topics which I find very cool, I made a decision to pursue physics in high school and beyond college. Now, at that point a thought formed in my head which noticed that I have passion for both computer science and physics, so I made another subsequent decision to pursue in both majors(degrees because CS and Physics Bachelor of Arts are in different colleges, which I will explain later.) There are many sources of information about this journey I have left out, but you a least have a gist of the kind of background I came from going into academia at my current university UC Berkeley.

&emsp;With this out of the way, you now might be wondering “Well why are you spending effort reading ML books outside of your classes when you can do it later in your future classes?”. The reasoning behind this is that AI is doable to learn because it’s such a broad topic that if you know enough college-level statistics(A little more than the content learned from AP Statistics for those who know), you can get started on learning different models people use to predict different things under very specific conditions and assumptions, which is the heart of AI models. One of the big points of using AI is to systematically predict what happened or will happen or how things would be grouped together.(Which you may notice is contrary to the goal of improving LLMs such as ChatGPT, Claude, etc, which all have an indirect goal to predict the response better in order to be a better helper of many subjects).

&emsp;Onto the spiel of how the college system works in an university, I want to make sure to first differentiate the difference between a university and a college. Typically, a university has many colleges, each housing their own departments. In my university’s case, that means we have the college of engineering, college of chemistry, college of letters and sciences, and so on. Each “college” essentially have their own requirements and specializations. A college by itself is usually much smaller and doesn’t have the same subgroup of departments contrary of universities. As for my current intended major, physics is considered to be in the college of letters and sciences and more specifically mathematical and physical sciences division(Which is a further subgroup since Letters and Sciences as a general category of majors have a diverse range of majors across sciences and humanities). On the other hand, Computer Science as a Bachelors of Arts is in the college of computing, data science, and society(CDSS), which also includes data science majors. Essentially my plan is to pursue a degree from both colleges, which is pretty common among the people who I know in UC Berkeley.
