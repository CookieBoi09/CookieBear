“I’d rather be ‘cooked’ by one of the best institutions than be doing the best in other institutions”

Yeah, maybe I said that a little too fast.

Jokes aside, I would like to share a humbling experience that might change the trajectory of how I approach academics in universities in general. Coming into UC Berkeley from a high school that has relatively high grade inflation, my brain was prepared for the uphill battle of “having to maintain a high-accuracy performance”, but my ego was not prepared for the reality check that sometimes greater effort does not equate to “significant” potential of getting a good score. At the same time, that doesn’t mean I shouldn’t try as hard because then I’m losing the opportunity to learn a lot of cool ideas within the semester. Through the whole summer semester, I was optimizing for maximum problem solves in both CS61A and CS61B, which costed me the mental capacity to make sure I have deep understanding of the material. This brings me to the next point, which is that “what” you are optimizing is oftentimes more important than the “magnitude” of your optimization. Consider this problem you may have seen in your calculus class where you are trying to maximize the area of a rectangle using a fixed perimeter. You can try to increase the length, but that costs you the width to a degree where you are getting less area than before. In my case, if I spent 30% of my time reviewing content and 70% practicing versus 15% review and 85% practicing, I would have scored much higher in my exams. At the end of the day, while I wasn’t expecting the results that I wanted given my full on effort in these two classes, what should matter is whether you actually learned ideas and concepts. You could take a class, get easy A, and learn absolutely nothing, which could be beneficial for your gpa, but your gpa is a wishy washy metric of how well you are grasping your material. Is it completely bogus? Of course not. But that doesn’t mean you should put your self-worth on some metric that only cares about results than the effort you have put into the class. We should not focus on the exact y value but rather the higher-order derivatives that dictate our future progress. All these statements from the surface level do sound like coping, but I’d rather not be in the delusional space of thinking that my gpa is the determining factor of my future success.
